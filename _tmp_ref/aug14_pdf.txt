RAVEN-MCS: Target-Risk-Calibrated
Asynchronous Federated Data Completion for
Mobile Crowdsensing under Two-Stage

**** and Anfeng Liu
Abstract—Mobile crowdsensing (MCS) increasingly relies on
federated and asynchronous learning to complete sparse spa-
tiotemporal data without centralizing raw observations. However,
the data that reach the server are shaped by a two-stage non-
random selection process containing three distinct layers—mobile
opportunity, actual observation, and usable-update delivery—
so the distribution of arriving updates need not match the
public deployment target. We propose RAVEN-MCS (Risk Align-
ment Via Exposure-aware Normalization for Mobile CrowdSens-
ing), which formulates asynchronous federated data completion
as a deployment-target risk-alignment problem. RAVEN-MCS
first separates deployment-target risk from arrival risk, then
reconstructs target-relevant mass by explicitly correcting the
opportunity, observation, and usable-update layers under legal
pre-outcome information, and finally maps the reconstructed
reference to a feasible aggregation decision through instantaneous
calibration and long-term target debt. We show that unequal
deployment and arrival laws induce non-interchangeable risk
functionals in general, that the ideal normalized sequential
correction recovers the compatible target law under explicit
support and identification conditions, and that the calibra-
tion program admits a unique global minimizer under the
stated convexity assumptions. Experiments show that structured
two-stage selection produces a clear target-versus-arrival risk
mismatch. On the scientifically comparable SensorScope and
U-Air blocks, RAVEN-MCS reduces deployment-group/client–
stratum structural mismatch by 3.96%/2.01% and 2.62%/2.28%,
respectively, relative to FedAvg, while maintaining competitive
deployment-target prediction risk with selective significant gains.
These results indicate that asynchronous MCS completion should
explicitly align selected training mass with the deployment target
rather than treating successfully delivered updates as an implicit
proxy for it.
Index Terms—Mobile crowdsensing, federated learning, data
completion, asynchronous learning, non-random selection, selec-
tion bias, target-risk alignment.
I. INTRODUCTION
Mobile crowdsensing (MCS) exploits the sensing, com-
munication, and computing capabilities of mobile devices
to collect large-scale urban and environmental data without
deploying a dense dedicated sensing infrastructure. Because
continuous sensing is costly and mobile participation is in-
trinsically intermittent, practical MCS systems observe only

a sparse subset of the desired spatiotemporal field. This has
motivated extensive work on sparse sensing and data com-
pletion, including robust cell selection, spatiotemporal corre-
lation modeling, and multi-scale inference [1]–[5]. Federated
learning (FL) further makes it possible to train completion
models without centralizing raw client records [6], and recent
work has combined federated completion with asynchronous
aggregation to accommodate irregular mobile data and stale
client updates [7], [8].
A less visible issue arises before aggregation: the data
that reach the server are not necessarily sampled from the
distribution that the deployed predictor is intended to serve.
Consider an atomic sensing target i. A mobile client must first
have an opportunity to cover i; the target must then actually be
observed; finally, the client’s local update must complete com-
putation and communication before the server’s usable-update
deadline. Thus, the two-stage MCS process contains three
distinct selection layers: mobile opportunity R, observation O,
and usable update U. Their probabilities depend on mobility,
sensing conditions, resource state, network quality, deadlines,
and staleness. Even if every individual layer is operationally
reasonable, their composition can make the distribution of
observed and successfully delivered data differ from the public
deployment target. Optimizing the distribution induced by
arriving updates can therefore optimize the wrong population
risk.
Existing work leaves an important gap at this point. Sparse
MCS completion primarily asks how to infer missing values
from the available observations [2], [9]; asynchronous feder-
ated completion asks how to learn effectively from irregular or
stale client updates [7], [8]; and participation-aware FL stud-
ies partial, arbitrary, or biased client participation [10]–[14].
These directions establish that sparsity, asynchrony, and non-
uniform participation matter, but they do not by themselves
resolve the following MCS problem: how should one align
asynchronous federated data completion with a deployment
target when sensing-side availability and update-side usability
jointly select the data that enter learning? In particular, cor-
recting only the final client-participation distribution does not
identify whether the distortion arose from mobile opportunity,
actual observation, or usable-update censoring; conversely,
reconstructing a statistically correct target mass does not imply
that this mass is realizable by the clients available in a finite
mobile window.

To address this gap, we develop RAVEN-MCS, short
for Risk Alignment Via Exposure-aware Normalization
for Mobile CrowdSensing. RAVEN-MCS treats deployment-
target alignment, rather than raw update frequency, as the ref-
erence semantics of asynchronous federated data completion.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the method first separates the public
deployment target from the arrival/usable-update law. It then
reconstructs target-relevant mass by explicitly factoring the
opportunity, observation, and usable-update layers under their
legal pre-outcome information boundaries. Finally, because
the reconstructed statistical target may be unattainable in a
particular mobile window, RAVEN-MCS maps the corrected
reference to a feasible aggregation decision through instanta-
neous target calibration, a strongly convex calibration program,
and long-term target debt. This separation yields three distinct
questions: what target should be served, what target mass the
selected data represent, and what target composition can be
feasibly realized now.
Our main contributions are as follows.
• Deployment-target risk semantics under two-stage
non-random selection. We formulate asynchronous fed-
erated MCS completion as a deployment-target alignment
problem rather than an arrival-centered learning problem.
The formulation distinguishes the atomic deployment law
from the law induced by mobile opportunity, observation,
and usable-update selection, and defines structural mis-
match at both deployment-group and client–opportunity-
stratum levels. We prove that when the deployment and
arrival laws differ, their induced risk functionals are not
generally interchangeable. This provides the formal basis
for asking whether a model is trained for the population
it is actually intended to serve.
• Selection-layer-aware target reconstruction. We de-
velop an exposure-aware normalized reconstruction that
keeps the opportunity/design, observation, and usable-
update mechanisms separate instead of collapsing them
into a single missingness probability. The correction
uses only legal pre-outcome information, combines local
H´ajek normalization with usable-update correction, and
yields a selection-corrected reference mass for aggre-
gation. Under explicit support, sequential ignorability,
correct ideal selection laws, and cluster-level regularity,
we show that the ideal pooled normalized sequential
estimator recovers the compatible deployment target.
The theory also makes clear that active clipping is a
finite-sample stabilization device rather than an exact-
unbiasedness claim.
• Feasibility-aware instantaneous and long-term tar-
get calibration. We show that statistically reconstructed
target mass and instantaneous mobile realizability are
different problems. RAVEN-MCS therefore calibrates the
corrected reference inside the current feasible aggrega-
tion set while accounting for target mismatch, reference
fidelity, uncertainty, and staleness, and carries unresolved
group under-service through a long-term target-debt pro-
cess. The resulting calibration program has a nonempty
compact convex feasible set and a unique global min-
imizer under the stated conditions; the debt recursion
further yields a deterministic prefix-level target-mismatch
accounting bound.
We evaluate these contributions using a balanced no-harm
control and three frozen experimental blocks organized around
the same causal chain. First, the NSW-Traffic experiment
shows that the same structured selection shift changes arrival-
weighted and deployment-target-weighted risks by materially
different amounts: across five methods, the paired Daligned =
∆RMSEµ −∆RMSEρ is consistently about −0.021, sup-
porting the risk-semantic motivation rather than any method-
specific dominance. Second, on the scientifically comparable
SensorScope and U-Air blocks, RAVEN-MCS consistently
reduces both structural mismatch measures relative to the
same-backbone baselines; compared with FedAvg, the re-
ductions in ∆group/∆c-s are 3.96%/2.01% on SensorScope
and 2.62%/2.28% on U-Air. Prediction risk remains com-
petitive rather than universally superior, with selective sig-
nificant gains, including a 2.04% reduction in deployment-
target RMSE relative to TwoStage-Hajek on U-Air. Third, the
ablation study shows that the correction and calibration com-
ponents have endpoint- and dataset-dependent roles: usable-
update correction has no clear isolated marginal effect in
the tested family, while target debt produces a statistically
consistent but quantitatively small structural correction. These
results match the intended claim: RAVEN-MCS primarily im-
proves alignment with the deployment target while preserving
competitive prediction quality.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion II reviews sparse MCS completion, asynchronous fed-
erated completion, and participation-aware FL. Section III
formulates the two-stage, three-layer selection process and
deployment-target risk. Sections IV and V present selection-

respectively. Section VI gives the theoretical analysis. Sec-
tion VII reports the evaluation, Section VIII discusses claim
and modeling boundaries, and Section IX concludes the paper.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Sparse Mobile Crowdsensing and Data Completion
Sparse mobile crowdsensing reduces sensing cost by col-
lecting only part of the spatiotemporal field and inferring
the unsensed portion. A substantial line of work therefore
focuses on exploiting spatial and temporal structure to improve

completion accuracy. Wang et al. [9] combine graph modeling
with spatiotemporal learning for joint urban inference and
prediction, while their outlier-aware completion method [1]
exploits both intra- and inter-data correlations to mitigate the
effect of anomalous observations. Li et al. [2] jointly study
adaptive region division, robust inference, and cost-effective
cell selection under sparse noise. More recent approaches
model the evolutionary structure of sparse observations [3],
examine how sparse completion affects downstream MCS
tasks [4], or exploit cross-scale correlations through a spa-
tiotemporal pyramid [5]. These studies substantially advance
completion under sparse, noisy, task-aware, or multi-scale
sensing, but their main objective remains the quality or down-
stream effect of data completion rather than deployment-target
risk under sequential selection. In contrast, RAVEN-MCS asks
a different question: when the data that become available
are themselves produced by non-random mobile opportunity
and observation processes, which population risk does the
resulting training mass represent? This distinction motivates
our explicit separation between the deployment target h(i) and
the opportunity stratum s(i), as well as the deployment-target
and arrival-risk semantics in Section III.
B. Federated and Asynchronous Data Completion
Federated learning (FL) enables distributed clients to train a
shared model without centralizing their raw data, with FedAvg
providing the canonical model-averaging formulation [6]. This
paradigm is particularly attractive for MCS because sensing
records are naturally distributed across mobile participants.
Most closely related to our setting, Wang et al. [7] develop
a federated distributed data-completion framework for sparse
MCS, combining local completion models with a time-aware
architecture and asynchronous aggregation to accommodate
irregular client data. In the broader asynchronous-FL litera-
ture, staleness-controlled aggregation has been used to trade
accuracy against system efficiency under heterogeneous client
speeds [8]. These works establish that privacy, distributed
sparsity, and stale updates can be handled without reverting
to centralized completion. Their primary focus, however, is
how to learn effectively from the updates that participate or
arrive. RAVEN-MCS instead additionally models how sensing
opportunities, actual observations, and usable asynchronous
updates sequentially determine which target mass reaches the
server. It therefore separates statistical target reconstruction
from the subsequent feasibility-aware aggregation decision
rather than treating asynchronous arrivals themselves as the
implicit deployment population.
C. Non-Uniform Participation and Selection Bias in FL
A complementary FL literature studies partial and non-
uniform client participation. Wang and Ji [10] provide a unified
convergence analysis for arbitrary participation patterns. Fed-
VARP [11] reduces the optimization variance caused by partial
participation using server-side surrogate updates, whereas an-
chor sampling [12] is designed to improve learning under par-
tial client participation and data heterogeneity. FedAU [13] is
particularly relevant: it estimates adaptive aggregation weights
from participation history so that heterogeneous, unknown
participation does not silently replace the original FL objective
by a participation-weighted one. More directly related to
temporal dependence, Sun et al. [14] show that non-uniform,
correlated client availability can bias FedAvg and develop a
debiasing method for correlated participation.
These results make clear that participation cannot always
be treated as uniform random sampling and, in FedAU in
particular, that participation can change the effective FL
objective. RAVEN-MCS differs in the object being aligned
and in the selection process being modeled: the reference
is an application-level deployment target, while selection
occurs before and after local sensing rather than only at
client/update participation. The problem studied here combines
three selection layers within a two-stage MCS process: mobile
opportunity R, observation O, and usable update U. RAVEN-
MCS further fixes a deployment target independently of this
arrival process, reconstructs target-relevant mass under stage-
specific legal information boundaries, and then calibrates
the reconstructed reference within the time-varying feasible
client support. Thus, the distinguishing point is not inverse
weighting or asynchronous aggregation in isolation, but the
joint deployment-target risk semantics–selection-layer-aware
reconstruction–feasibility-aware calibration chain required by
mobile crowdsensing under non-random sensing and update
availability.

D. Information Boundary and Stabilization
Every correction applied in window r is measurable before
its own random outcome. Opportunity estimates use lagged op-
portunity history; observation propensities exclude the current
sensing outcome and label; usable-update propensities exclude
the realized U outcome and future events; and the server
determines αr without inspecting the coordinates, norm, or di-

that update’s weight. The clipping constants in (36) and (47)

in Section VI concerns the ideal ratios, or equivalently regimes
in which the stabilizing bounds are inactive on target support.

E. Implications for RAVEN-MCS
The theoretical chain now matches the three-part design.
Proposition 1 formalizes why a usable-update law cannot
replace the deployment target by convention. Proposition 2
shows that, under explicit support, sequential ignorability,
correct ideal selection laws, and cluster-level regularity, the
layered opportunity–observation–usable correction has a co-
herent target-law interpretation. Theorem 1 establishes that
the resulting corrected reference can be mapped to a unique
feasible aggregation decision in every active window. Together
with the debt accounting relation (60), these results support
RAVEN’s target-alignment mainline without claiming univer-
sal predictive superiority or guarantees outside the supported,
conditionally identifiable regime.
VII. EVALUATION
We evaluate RAVEN-MCS around the three questions im-
plied by the theory in Sections III–VI, rather than around
individual implementation modules.
• RQ1: Does two-stage non-random selection create a
meaningful deployment-target risk mismatch?
• RQ2: Does RAVEN-MCS reduce structural deployment-
target mismatch while maintaining competitive target
prediction risk?
• RQ3: How do the core correction and calibration com-
ponents contribute to RAVEN’s structural and predictive
behavior?
E1 is used only as a balanced no-harm control. E2 addresses
RQ1 by comparing arrival-weighted and deployment-target-
weighted risks under controlled two-stage selection. E3 ad-
dresses RQ2 on the two scientifically comparable SensorScope
and U-Air blocks. E4 addresses RQ3 by removing one cor-
rection or calibration component at a time. This organization
mirrors the theoretical chain: Proposition 1 distinguishes the
two risk semantics, Proposition 2 gives the target-law inter-
pretation of ideal sequential reconstruction, and Section V
maps the reconstructed reference to a feasible aggregation
decision while tracking the deployment target under finite-
window mobile support.

A. Experimental Setup
1) Datasets and target semantics: Table I summarizes the
evaluated datasets. SensorScope and U-Air provide the main
E3/E4 evidence. For both, the deployment-target mapping h(i)
consists of four UTC time-of-day groups (00–06, 06–12, 12–
18, and 18–24), whose masses are frozen from the training
split without test-label values. Their opportunity strata s(i) are
finer: spatial identity × four time blocks × weekday/weekend.
Accordingly, h(i) and s(i) retain the distinct semantics re-
quired by Section III-B. SensorScope contains 55 station-level
spatial identities and at most 440 opportunity strata, whereas
U-Air contains 36 spatial identities and at most 288 strata.
NSW-Traffic contains 43 stations and uses four frozen
coordinate-based regions; its opportunity strata combine re-
gion, time block, and weekday/weekend information, yielding
at most 32 strata. We use this dataset for the sealed E2
risk-semantic experiment. We do not use its cross-vintage
E3 outputs for method-level inference because the reused E2
execution path and the later E3 path are not scientifically
homogeneous.
2) Selection protocol and compared methods: The main
E3/E4 condition is Complete-aligned, in which opportunity,
observation, and usable-update selection are all active and
non-random in the same direction. All compared methods
within a paired block consume the same frozen Event-
Trace, so they face identical opportunity, observation, registra-
tion, and update-availability events. The five principal same-
backbone baselines are FedAvg-Window, FedAsync-Window,
TimeAlign-Agg, Local-Hajek, and TwoStage-Hajek. We ad-
ditionally report Inst-Cal and Debt-Cal to expose the transi-
tion from reconstruction to calibration. Central-All, Central-
Delivered, and RAVEN-SimOracle are reference-only methods
and are not included in the same inferential family as the
distributed baselines.
E2 uses 20 paired seeds for each method and scenario.
E3 uses 20 paired seeds per scientifically comparable dataset
and method. E4 uses the same 20 seeds for full RAVEN and
each one-factor ablation. The balanced E1 sanity control uses
five frozen seeds. No test result is used to tune the selection
process, method parameters, target groups, or opportunity
strata.
3) Metrics and statistical policy: Prediction is measured
under the deployment target by RMSEµ; Tail RMSE mea-
sures the corresponding frozen tail-group error. For RQ1
only, RMSEρ uses the atomic arrival law in (22). Structural
alignment is measured by ∆group in (28) and ∆c-s in (30).
Smaller values are better for all four main metrics.
All formal method comparisons are paired by seed. We use
the frozen paired Wilcoxon protocol and apply Holm correc-
tion within the predeclared comparison families at α = 0.05;
absolute differences below EPS_ZERO = 10−12 are treated
as numerical ties. Tables report mean ± standard deviation.
For P2, warning-affected allocations satisfy the reconstructable
frozen feasibility constraints; the sealed outputs do not support
retrospective certification of exact numerical optimality, and
we make no such empirical claim.
B. RQ1: Does Two-Stage Selection Change the Relevant Risk?

VIII. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
The evaluation supports a deliberately narrower claim than
universal predictive superiority. RAVEN-MCS is designed
to correct a distributional mismatch: mobile opportunities,
observations, and usable updates determine which data reach
learning, whereas the deployed predictor is judged under a
separate public target law. The most consistent empirical effect
is therefore a reduction in structural target mismatch, while the
resulting change in prediction error can be smaller and dataset
dependent. This section discusses that distinction and the main
boundaries of the present evidence.
A. Why Structural Alignment Need Not Imply Universal RMSE
Gains
The separation between structural alignment and immediate
prediction error is central to interpreting RQ2. Proposition 1
establishes that the arrival and deployment-target laws define
different risk functionals in general, but it does not state that
every predictor must incur a large numerical gap between
them. Likewise, Proposition 2 identifies the target interpre-
tation of the ideal pooled sequential correction; it does not

===== PAGE 12 =====
12
guarantee that every finite-window feasible client set can
realize the target exactly. The calibration program in Section V
is needed precisely because mobile support is time varying and
can make the target instantaneously unattainable.
Consequently, a reduction in ∆group or ∆c-s need not
translate one-for-one into a reduction in RMSEµ. Structural
correction changes which parts of the deployment target are
represented by the effective training mass, whereas final pre-
diction error also depends on the finite sample, the predictive
model class, local noise, and the optimization trajectory. A
method can therefore move the effective training distribution
closer to the intended target while producing only a small
RMSE change. Conversely, a particular ablation can obtain a
lower finite-sample RMSE while moving one structural rep-
resentation farther from its target. The U-Air Design ablation
in Table III is the clearest instance of this trade-off.
For this reason, the structural metrics are not auxiliary
fairness or diagnostic scores. They directly measure the ob-
jective introduced in Section III-E. At the same time, RMSEµ
remains essential because structural alignment is useful only
insofar as the final predictor remains competitive under the
deployment target. The appropriate empirical conclusion from
Section VII is therefore consistent structural correction with
competitive deployment-target prediction risk and selective
predictive gains, rather than universal RMSE dominance.
B. RAVEN as a Coupled Correction System
The ablations also caution against interpreting Design, Obs,
Use, Inst, and Debt as five independently additive improve-
ments. Their roles occur at different points in the pipeline.
Design and Obs change the statistical interpretation of the
sensing-side mass; Use addresses update-side censoring; Inst
maps the reconstructed reference into the currently feasible
aggregation set; and Debt carries unresolved group under-
service across windows. Because these terms act on different
representations and constraints, their effects can change sign
across endpoints.
This interpretation is consistent with RQ3. Design has the
clearest effect on the finer client–opportunity-stratum represen-
tation, but can trade that alignment against group-level or pre-
diction endpoints. Obs has smaller, endpoint-specific structural
effects. The isolated marginal effect of Use is not established
in the tested Complete-aligned family, so the experiments
do not support calling usable-update correction empirically
indispensable. This does not collapse the theoretical distinction
between observation and usable update: the two variables have
different conditioning histories in Section III-B. It only means
that, under the current sealed E4 regime, removing the Use
factor does not create a robust measurable change in the four
core endpoints.
Similarly, Debt should not be sold as a large standalone per-
formance driver. Its removal produces a statistically consistent
but quantitatively small increase in structural mismatch. This
is compatible with its intended role: the recursion in (59) is a
long-term accounting mechanism, and the deterministic bound
in (60) relates accumulated debt to prefix target mismatch. The
experiments do not establish the stronger condition
∥QR∥1 = o(SR),
(75)
and therefore do not empirically prove asymptotic vanishing
of ∆group.
C. Statistical and Modeling Boundaries
The theoretical guarantees rely on explicit support and
sequential identification conditions. In particular, A1 requires
positivity on target-supported events, and A2 requires the
observation and usable-update mechanisms to be condition-
ally mean-ignorable given their legal pre-outcome histories.
RAVEN therefore does not identify target mass that is struc-
turally outside opportunity support, nor does the present the-
ory guarantee correction under arbitrary hidden confounding.
These are modeling boundaries rather than implementation
details.
The exact reconstruction statement also concerns the ideal
untruncated ratios under A3. The implemented ratios are
clipped and use estimated propensities to control finite-sample
weight concentration. When clipping is active, the practical
estimator deliberately trades exact inverse-selection identifica-
tion for stability; Section VI does not claim exact unbiasedness
in that regime. Similarly, the target mappings h(i) and s(i)
are assumed to be public or training-side constructions frozen
before test-label evaluation. If the deployment objective itself
is misspecified, target calibration can faithfully track the wrong
target; RAVEN does not solve target-definition error.

Broader claims would require evidence outside the present
study and are not made here.
IX. CONCLUSION
This paper studied asynchronous federated data completion
in mobile crowdsensing from a deployment-target perspective.
The key observation is that the data reaching the server are
shaped sequentially by mobile opportunity, actual observation,
and usable-update selection; consequently, the arrival/usable-
update distribution need not represent the distribution under
which the deployed predictor should be evaluated. RAVEN-
MCS addresses this mismatch through three coordinated steps:
it formulates deployment-target risk separately from arrival
risk, reconstructs target-relevant mass with selection-layer-
aware normalized correction, and maps the reconstructed
reference to a feasible asynchronous aggregation decision
using instantaneous calibration and long-term target debt.
The accompanying analysis shows that, when the deployment
and arrival laws differ, their induced risk functionals are not
generally interchangeable; it also establishes the target-law
interpretation of the ideal normalized sequential correction
under explicit support and identification conditions and the
existence and uniqueness of the feasible calibration solution
under the stated convexity conditions.
The experiments support the same claim structure. Under
structured two-stage non-random selection, arrival-weighted
and deployment-target-weighted risks respond differently, val-
idating the need to distinguish the two semantics. On the scien-
tifically comparable SensorScope and U-Air blocks, RAVEN-
MCS consistently reduces structural deployment-target mis-
match while maintaining competitive target prediction risk,
with selective significant predictive gains rather than universal
RMSE superiority. The component analysis further shows that
the correction and calibration stages form a coupled system:
their effects are endpoint and dataset dependent, usable-update
correction has no clear isolated effect in the tested ablation
family, and target debt provides a statistically consistent but
quantitatively small structural correction.
Overall, the results suggest that asynchronous MCS data
completion should not treat the distribution of successfully
delivered updates as an implicit proxy for the deployment
objective. Explicitly separating what target should be served,
what mass the selected data represent, and what target com-
position is currently realizable provides a principled basis for
aligning federated completion with deployment requirements
under time-varying mobile availability.

F. Proof-Obligation Summary
The four completed arguments establish the exact scope of
the theory used in the main text:
1) Proposition 1 proves that unequal deployment and arrival
laws define different risk functionals in general.
2) Proposition 2 proves consistency of the ideal normal-
ized sequential opportunity–observation–usable correc-
tion under A1–A3 and explicitly connects the recon-
structed client–atomic law to both πtar
k,s and µ through
the frozen target compatibility relation.
3) Theorem 1 proves existence and uniqueness of the
solution to (P2) under A4.
4) The debt recursion gives a deterministic long-term ac-
counting bound; vanishing structural mismatch follows
whenever debt grows sublinearly in active step mass.
None of these results asserts identification outside target
support, robustness to arbitrary hidden confounding, exact
unbiasedness under active clipping, or universal predictive
superiority.
